Introduction To ANOVA

Analysis of Variance

  • Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is a collection of statistical techniques for examining group differences
  • ANOVA extends the methods covered so far to more complex experimental designs:
    • a single factor (i.e., independent variable) with three or more levels (single-factor designs, Weeks 1-4; Mark)
    • two or more factors within the same design (factorial designs, Weeks 5-8; Emma)

  • Today: single-factor between-participants ANOVA

Limitations Of The t-Test

A t-test allows us to compare two means

  • Many experiments involve more than two treatment groups
  • Multiple t-tests could be us to compare the groups ...
  • ... but conducting multiple tests inflates the probability of a Type I error
  • ANOVA allows us to test for differences among multiple means simultaneously

Sources Of Variability In Raw Data

Recap: Sources of Variability in Between-Participants Designs

  • ​In a between-participants experiment, scores vary between groups and within groups
  • Between-group variability: refers to differences between the group means
  • Within-group variability: refers to differences among scores within each group
  • Detecting a treatment effect depends on the size of the between-group differences relative to the within-group variability

Individual Differences → Primarily Within-Group Variability

  • Participants naturally differ in characteristics that influence their scores
  • Because different participants make up each treatment condition, their scores will vary even when they receive the same treatment
  • Individual differences therefore contribute to within-group variability
  • Large individual differences can make a treatment effect harder to detect

  1. Treatment effects

Random (Residual) Errors → Primarily Within-Group Variability

  • Scores can also vary because of unpredictable factors unrelated to either the treatment or stable individual differences
  • Examples might include:
    • varying external conditions (e.g., time of day, temperature)
    • state of the participant (e.g., current focus of attention)
    • experimenter's ability to measure and score accurately
  • Because these factors can affect participants within the same treatment condition, they contribute to within-group variability

...Experimental Errors

  • Collectively, individual differences and random errors are known as experimental errors
  • The aim of every experiment is to minimise experimental errors, so they do not obscure treatment effects
  • Such errors spread data away from the group means, increasing the variability in scores and their standard deviations
  • Larger standard deviations = more experimental error

The F Ratio For A Between-Participants Design

The Logic of ANOVA

  • ANOVA evaluates treatment effects by comparing:
    • the variability between groups
    • with the experimental error
  • If the treatment has an effect, we expect the variability between groups to be relatively large compared with experimental error
F = \frac{\textrm{between-group variance}}{\textrm{experimental error}}

For A Between-Participants ANOVA

F = \frac{\textrm{between-group variance}}{\textrm{within-group variance}}
F = \frac{\textrm{treatment effects + experimental error}}{\textrm{experimental error}}
F = \frac{\textrm{treatment effects + individual differences + random errors}}{\textrm{individual differences + random errors}}

Testing For Differences

  • Under H0, the samples come from the same population
  •                               [No difference in the population means]
  • Experimental effect = 0
  • All differences are due to individual differences + random (residual) errors
H_{0} \textrm{the Null Hypothesis}
\mu_{1} = \mu_{2} = \mu_{3}

Testing For Differences

  • Under H1, the samples come from different populations
  • At least one population mean differs
  • Experimental effect > 0
  • Differences are due to individual differences, random (residual) errors and the treatment effects
H_{1} \textrm{the Alternative Hypothesis}

If The Null Hypothesis Is True...

F \approx \frac{0 + \textrm{experimental error}}{\textrm{experimental error}} \approx 1
  • Under H0, the treatment effect is zero
  • Between-group and within-group variability therefore reflect the same experimental error
  • An F-ratio close to 1 is what we would expect if H0 were true

If The Alternative Hypothesis Is True...

F = \frac{\textrm{treatment effects + experimental error}}{\textrm{experimental error}} > 1
  • Under H1, the treatment contributes additional between-group variability:
  • As the treatment effect increases relative to experimental error, the F-ratio becomes larger
  • Overall, when looking for a treatment effect, you are looking for a relatively large F value

Assumptions of ANOVA

Why Does ANOVA Have Assumptions?

  • ANOVA allows us to use our sample data to draw conclusions about populations
  • To do this, the F-test makes certain assumptions about our data
  • If these assumptions are seriously violated, our statistical conclusions may be less trustworthy
  • Fortunately, ANOVA is reasonably robust to modest violations of some assumptions

Before interpreting an ANOVA, we should consider whether its assumptions are reasonably satisfied

Assumptions Of ANOVA

1. Independence of Observations

  • Each observation should be independent of the others
  • A participant's score should not influence or depend upon another participant's score
  • In a between-participants design, each participant should contribute data to only one treatment condition
  • Independence is primarily ensured through good experimental design

2. Normality

  • Scores should be approximately normally distributed within each treatment group
  • The assumption concerns the distributions within groups, not simply the distribution of all scores combined
  • Small departures from normality are generally less concerning than substantial skew or extreme observations

3. Homogeneity of Variance

  • The amount of variability should be reasonably similar across treatment groups
  • In other words, one group should not be much more variable than another
  • This is the assumption of homogeneity of variance

Homogeneity does not mean that the group means must be equal — it concerns the spread of scores within the groups.

Assumptions and Rogue Data

  • Normality and homogeneity of variance can both be affected by unusual or extreme observations
  • Before conducting an ANOVA, we therefore need to examine our data for potential problems
  • This brings us to the issue of rogue data and outliers

Rogue Data and Outliers

When Data Causes Problems

  • Real datasets do not always conform neatly to the assumptions of ANOVA
  • Problems can include skewed distributions, unequal variances, and extreme observations
  • Before analysing the data, we should consider why the problem has occurred
  • Possible responses include transforming the data, dealing appropriately with outliers, or—in more serious cases—using a different analysis

Transforming Data

  • Transformations change the scale on which the data are expressed
  • They can sometimes reduce skew, lessen the influence of extreme scores, or improve homogeneity of variance
  • Common examples include logarithmic, square-root, and reciprocal transformations
  • The transformed scores—not the original scores—are then used in the analysis

Transforming Data

Limitations of Transformations

  • A transformation does not guarantee that assumption violations will disappear
  • It may not help when different groups are skewed in different directions
  • Transformations make results less intuitive to interpret because the analysis is conducted on a different scale
  • Because ANOVA is relatively robust, transformation is not automatically necessary for modest departures from its assumptions

Outliers

  • An outlier is an observation that is unusually distant from the other observations
  • Outliers may arise from measurement/recording errors, lapses in attention, or genuine individual differences
  • Extreme scores can disproportionately affect the mean and variance
  • An extreme observation should not automatically be deleted

Outliers

Dealing With Outliers

  • Check the observation: is it a data-entry, coding, or measurement error?
  • Consider whether there is a substantive explanation for the extreme value
  • Use consistent, defensible criteria when identifying potential outliers
  • Any exclusions should be justified and reported transparently

In the practical: we will look at how to identify and deal with potential outliers in R

What If The Data Are Problematic?

  • First, consider why the data are problematic

  • Check for data errors and extreme observations, and decide how they should be handled

  • In some circumstances, a data transformation may improve the properties of the data

  • Alternatively, we can use a non-parametric test, which makes fewer assumptions about the distribution of the data

  • For a single-factor between-participants design, one alternative is the Kruskal–Wallis test

Pairwise Comparisons

Why Not Just Run Multiple t-Tests?

Two Approaches to Follow Up Comparisons

1. Planned comparisons

  • Comparisons specified before examining the results
  • Used to test particular hypotheses

2. Post-hoc comparisons

  • Comparisons decided upon after examining the results
  • Used to explore where differences lie

Do Planned Comparisons Need a Correction?

  • Multiple comparisons increase the familywise Type I error rate
  • Keep the number of planned comparisons as low as possible
  • A useful rule of thumb is
\boxed{\text{Maximum planned comparisons}=a-1}
  • With three levels:
3-1=2\text{ planned comparisons}

With a small number of genuinely planned comparisons, we can test each at the usual \(\alpha=.05\). With larger numbers, we may need to adjust our significance level

When Might We Use Bonferroni?

  • With larger numbers of comparisons, familywise Type I error becomes increasingly problematic
  • One simple way to control this is the Bonferroni correction 
  • Divide the desired familywise significance level by the number of comparisons:
\alpha_{\text{adjusted}} = \frac{\alpha}{\text{number of comparisons}}
\frac{.05}{5}=.01

With five comparisons, each test must therefore reach \(p<.01\).

Post-Hoc Comparisons

When Do We Use Post-Hoc Tests?

  • Sometimes we have no specific predictions about which means will differ
  • Following a significant omnibus ANOVA, we may want to determine where the differences lie
  • We therefore examine all possible pairwise comparisons
  • But making multiple comparisons increases the familywise Type I error rate
  • Post-hoc tests allow us to make these comparisons while controlling that error rate

Post-hoc tests examine all possible pairwise comparisons

Tukey-Kramer HSD

  • One commonly used post-hoc procedure is the Tukey–Kramer HSD
  • HSD stands for Honestly Significant Difference
  • It determines the minimum difference between two means required for statistical significance
  • It controls the familywise Type I error rate across the set of pairwise comparisons
\boxed{ \text{Observed difference between means} > W \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{significant} }

where \(W\) is the critical difference​ 

Calculating The Critical Difference

  • \(q\) = Studentized range statistic
  • \(r\) = number of means being compared
  • \(df_{\text{error}}\) = error degrees of freedom from the ANOVA
  • \(MS_{\text{error}}\) = within-group mean square from the ANOVA
  • \(n\) = number of observations in each group
W=q(r,df_{\text{error}}) \sqrt{\frac{MS_{\text{error}}}{n}}

Just like our planned comparisons, Tukey uses the estimate of experimental error from our ANOVA

Applying Tukey-Kramer HSD

  • From our ANOVA:

\[ r=3 \]\[ df_{\text{error}}=12 \]\[ MS_{\text{error}}=4 \]\[ n=5 \]
